**Date:** July 30, 2026

## 1. Executive Summary

This inquiry began with a simple question: can RT be used to organize breeding records so that inherited genetic relations can later be examined for recurring traits?

The first proposed application involved chicory breeding. RT was initially treated only as a journal structure in which two parent organisms were recorded as the aspects of a breeding relation and each offspring was recorded as a separate realized resolution:

[
RT_{\text{cross}}=(Parent_A\mid Parent_B)
]

[
RT_{\text{cross}}\rightarrow Offspring
]

The inquiry then moved from chicory to budding yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, because yeast provides a much more completely documented genetic system, a compact genome, controlled mating, haploid and diploid life stages, and naturally structured four-spore tetrads. The standard yeast nuclear genome contains 16 chromosomes, making it complex enough to be meaningful but small enough for complete computational tracking.

RecombineX was installed under Ubuntu 22.04 in WSL2 and used to generate one complete simulated recombinant yeast tetrad. RecombineX is designed for gamete genotyping, tetrad-based recombination analysis, and simulation of recombinant tetrads with declared crossover and noncrossover counts.

The first completed simulation demonstrated that RT can represent the complete inheritance progression:

[
(P1\mid P2)
\rightarrow
{Spore_a,Spore_b,Spore_c,Spore_d}
]

The simulation preserved parental origin, recombination events, linkage blocks, gene-conversion tracts, chromosome locations, and the resulting genotype of each spore.

The inquiry then expanded toward a more ambitious goal:

[
\text{ancestry + genotype + environment}
\rightarrow
\text{predicted dominant trait}
]

The central conclusion is that RT can plausibly serve as a relational journal and evidence map for dominance prediction, but dominance itself cannot be measured from haploid spores alone. Dominance must be evaluated in diploid organisms where two alleles coexist. The proposed next experiment therefore constructs diploid organisms from recombinant spores, records the allele pair at every locus, supplies controlled phenotype observations, maps trait-specific dominance relations, and predicts traits in organisms from held-out tetrad lineages.

No real biological dominance prediction has yet been demonstrated. The completed result is a validated inheritance journal for one simulated tetrad and a runnable design for the larger dominance experiment.

## 2. Original RT Breeding-Journal Concept

The initial concept treated RT conservatively.

For every offspring:

[
RT_O=(RT_{P_A}\mid RT_{P_B})
]

The two parent organisms are the two aspects participating in the production of the offspring. Each offspring is a distinct resolution even when two offspring share the same parents.

For example:

[
(P_A\mid P_B)\rightarrow O_1
]

[
(P_A\mid P_B)\rightarrow O_2
]

The journal does not initially claim:

* which genes caused an observed trait;
* which parent contributed the decisive effect;
* whether the offspring is superior;
* whether a trait is dominant;
* whether RT predicts biology better than established genetic methods.

Instead, it preserves:

* parent and offspring identities;
* inherited marker states;
* recombination history;
* phenotype observations;
* environmental conditions;
* supporting and opposing evidence;
* uncertainty and provenance.

The intended later use is many-to-one analysis:

[
{
RT_{O_1},
RT_{O_2},
\ldots,
RT_{O_n}
}
\rightarrow
RT_{\text{candidate region or trait relation}}
]

Many offspring records converge toward a bounded candidate conclusion, but the original records remain available for inspection.

## 3. Why the Inquiry Shifted from Chicory to Yeast

The chicory model was useful for clarifying the RT journal structure, but it required extensive invented genetics and synthetic trait rules. That created a risk that the simulation would merely rediscover assumptions placed into it.

Yeast offers a better test system because it provides:

* a fully sequenced and heavily annotated genome;
* 16 nuclear chromosomes;
* haploid organisms that expose individual alleles;
* diploid organisms in which dominance can be observed;
* controlled mating;
* meiosis producing four related spores;
* extensive public genotype and phenotype resources;
* existing recombination-analysis software.

The natural tetrad structure is especially compatible with RT.

A parental relation produces one diploid:

[
(P1\mid P2)\rightarrow Diploid_{P1/P2}

One meiosis then produces four distinguishable haploid outcomes:

[
Diploid_{P1/P2}
\rightarrow

This gives RT a biologically real one-to-many structure rather than an arbitrarily selected number of offspring.

## 9. Refinement of the Dominance Question

The inquiry initially described the goal as tracking “dominant traits along the RT progression.”

A necessary biological correction was identified:

> Dominance cannot be observed in a haploid organism because only one allele is present at each ordinary locus.

The four RecombineX spores are haploid. They can reveal:

* which parental allele was inherited;
* the effect of an allele when expressed alone;
* recombination and linkage;
* segregation and gene conversion.

They cannot alone reveal whether one allele dominates another.

Dominance requires a diploid genotype:

[
P1/P1,\qquad P1/P2,\qquad P2/P2
]

For a measured trait:

[
\mu_{11}=Phenotype(P1/P1)
]

[
\mu_{12}=Phenotype(P1/P2)
]

[
\mu_{22}=Phenotype(P2/P2)
]

The additive effect can be estimated as:

[
a=\frac{\mu_{11}-\mu_{22}}{2}
]

The dominance deviation is:

[
d=
\mu_{12}
--------

\frac{\mu_{11}+\mu_{22}}{2}
]

This permits classification as:

* additive;
* P1-dominant;
* P2-dominant;
* partially dominant;
* recessive;
* overdominant;
* underdominant.

The inquiry therefore shifted from asking whether an organism has “dominant traits” to asking:

> Given a particular allele pair, trait, genomic background and environment, which phenotype is expressed and how does the heterozygote differ from the two homozygotes?

Dominance is not treated as a permanent property of P1 or P2.

It is an RT-scoped relation:

[
Dominance=
Relation(
Allele_1,
Allele_2,
Trait,
GenomeBackground,
Environment
)
]
