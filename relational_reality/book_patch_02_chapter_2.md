# Chapter 2: Relational Asymmetry

### 2.1 From Isolated Persistence to Interaction
In the previous chapter, we established the minimal condition for a single process to persist: the NOT-Axiom. This recursive loop between distinguishability ($\mathcal{E}$) and admissible continuation ($\delta_a$) allows a localized process to "solve" the problem of its own continuity. However, an isolated process loop is limited in its complexity. The emergence of stable, macroscopic structures requires the interaction of multiple such loops.

When two or more continuation systems overlap, their individual demands for persistence begin to condition one another. We call this interactive coupling **procedural registration**, often represented by the operator $\Leftrightarrow_{xa}$. Interaction is not the movement of objects through a pre-existing $space\_app$; it is the process by which one continuation system's distinguishability becomes a constraint on another system's admissibility window ($A$). In this multi-process environment, distinguishability is no longer merely an internal state; it becomes a relational metric.

### 2.2 Relational Registration
We define the metric of this interaction as relational distinguishability, denoted by the symbol $D$. When two systems, $A$ and $B$, interact, we must consider how each registers the other. This registration is expressed as $D(A|B)$—the distinguishability of $A$ as registered by the procedural reach of $B$.

$$ D(A|B) \neq D(B|A) $$

Crucially, this registration is inherently directional. Because each system is a product of its own unique residue-history ($R$), the way system $B$ filters the presence of $A$ into its own admissibility window ($A$) is structurally different from how $A$ filters $B$. Relational distinguishability is not a distance in a spatial manifold; it is a measure of **procedural accessibility and mismatch pressure** between two distinct continuation histories.

### 2.3 Why Symmetry Collapses
A fundamental requirement for continued interaction is that this relational registration must remain asymmetric. If the interaction between two processes were to become perfectly symmetric—meaning $D(A|B) = D(B|A)$—the procedural directionality of both systems would effectively cancel out.

In our formal program, we refer to this as the **Symmetry Fracture** collapse mode:

$$ D_{12}=D_{21} \implies \nabla_{rel}\to0 $$

Perfect reciprocity leads to procedural inertness. Without a gradient of difference—a "push" or "pull" generated by the mismatch between how each system registers the other—there is no impetus for directional continuation. Symmetry, in this context, is a state of equilibrium that leads to the exhaustion of continuation pressure. Within this framework, pressure does not denote a primitive physical $force\_app$. It refers to unresolved admissibility-conditioned asymmetry requiring organizational resolution. Asymmetry is the procedural "tension" that keeps the engine of continuation running.

### 2.4 Residue-Conditioned Interaction
This persistent asymmetry is guaranteed by the residue-history ($R$) of each process. As established in the NOT-Axiom, every update leaves a trace that conditions future admissibility. When two processes interact, they do so through the lens of these accumulated traces.

No interaction is context-free. When system $B$ registers system $A$, it does so against the backdrop of its own $R$. Because no two processes share identical histories, their respective admissibility windows will always filter the same interaction in different ways. Interaction, therefore, is the recursive conditioning of residue-mediated continuation histories, and the resulting asymmetry drives the increasing complexity of admissible relational structure.

### 2.5 The Emergence of Directionality
From this structural asymmetry, a primitive form of directionality begins to emerge. This is not yet the directionality of a geometric vector in three-dimensional $space\_app$. Rather, it is **continuation tension**. 

If $A$ registers $B$ more strongly than $B$ registers $A$, there is a directional pressure in the update cycle. The systems must resolve this mismatch to maintain admissibility. Directional continuation emerges procedurally as a consequence of asymmetric reachability conditions rather than from pre-existing geometric axes. This tension forces the processes to seek "alignment"—not as a movement toward a point, but as a reconfiguration of their internal update rules to accommodate the presence of the other. This directional pressure is the precursor to orientation, the procedural necessity of knowing "which way" to continue when the relational landscape is no longer uniform.

### 2.6 Procedural Ordering
As interaction becomes asymmetric and directional, the order in which updates occur becomes structurally significant. We represent this as **procedural ordering** ($\prec$).

$$ x \prec x' $$

In an isolated loop, $x \prec x'$ is a simple cycle. In an interactive environment, the sequence of updates—how $A$ updates in response to $B$, which then conditions $A$'s next update—is non-commutative. Because residue-updates are non-commutative, procedural ordering cannot generally be reversed without altering admissibility conditions.

$$ \Phi_{t+1}\circ\Phi_t \neq \Phi_t\circ\Phi_{t+1} $$

The path taken through the relational landscape matters because each step modifies the residue $R$ and thus the future admissibility window ($A$). This non-commutativity establishes the "arrow" of the process, a structural ordering that will eventually project at macroscopic scales as the experience of $time\_app$.

### 2.7 Transition to Orientation
The persistence of relational asymmetry creates a permanent "gradient" in the procedural substrate. To maintain stable continuation in the face of this gradient, localized processes must develop a way to stabilize their directional update rules. They require a local reference for their asymmetry.

This necessity leads us to the emergence of local orientation references, which we represent as the orientation array $\{-(i)_\alpha\}$. Asymmetry demands that a process not only "continues" but continues "relative to" its interaction partners. To understand how these local references are established and how they eventually organize into the global manifold of $geometry\_app$, we must examine the formal structure of orientation. This will be the subject of Chapter 3: Orientation and Procedural Ordering.

### Scope Boundary Note
Relational asymmetry, as described here, is a procedural condition required for the persistence of interacting systems. It is not a discovered physical $force\_app$ such as electromagnetism or $gravity\_app$, though those phenomena may eventually be interpreted as macroscopic projections of these underlying asymmetries. Directionality in this chapter is relational and procedural, emerging from the necessity of non-null interaction gradients, and should not be confused with the geometric vectors of classical physics. The framework remains an exploratory and analogical mapping of process dynamics.
