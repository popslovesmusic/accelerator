# Chapter 13: Collapse, Identity Drift, and Procedural Failure

### 13.0 Formal Anchor: $A_{route}^{-} \rightarrow Drift_{recursive}$

Chapter 12 defined **recursive identity** as a distributed persistence structure stabilized by continuation-preserving arbitration: residue-conditioned admissibility at the route level remains sufficiently coherent to preserve repeated residue-coherent re-entry across routing arbitration cycles. This chapter derives the complementary result: persistence is conditional, and destabilization occurs when recursive admissibility reinforcement fails.

We maintain strict boundary discipline. Collapse, drift, and fragmentation in this chapter are **procedural failure modes** inside the framework: admissibility breakdown, residue fracture, and routing incoherence. No existential annihilation, metaphysical death, or psychological pathology claims are made or implied.

### 13.1 Persistence Is Conditional

Recursive identity persistence is a stabilized regime, not a guaranteed invariant. Stabilization requires that:
- admissible re-entry remains available under $A$ and reachability $I$,
- residue-history $R$ remains coherent enough to preserve routing scope persistence,
- the recursion condition for stabilization continues to remain sufficiently coherent under ordering $\prec$.

Preferred narrative compression (this chapter): **stabilized regime, not a guaranteed invariant**.

The knot anchor supplies the minimal stabilization threshold logic:
$$\oint_{recursive} \Delta_R \not> \Theta_D$$
When the recursive circulation of residue-change fails to exceed the distinguishability threshold, stabilization is no longer guaranteed. This does not imply “non-being.” It implies that the configuration no longer remains a stabilized recurrence basin under admissibility constraints.

### 13.2 Identity Drift

**Identity drift** is defined procedurally as gradual deviation of routing organization away from a previously stabilized residue-coherent recurrence basin. Drift is not an essence change; it is a change in what remains re-enterable under residue-conditioned admissibility.

The drift anchor is:
$$A_{route}^{-} \rightarrow Drift_{recursive}$$
Here $A_{route}^{-}$ denotes route-level admissibility that has degraded, narrowed, or become unstable relative to prior stabilization. Drift emerges when routing arbitration cycles increasingly fail to re-enter the same residue-coherent basin, producing a decreasing re-entry ratio and increasing mismatch in the residue signature across cycles.

Preferred narrative compression (this chapter): **change in what remains re-enterable**.

Drift therefore has three procedural signatures:
- **failed admissible re-entry:** re-entry candidates that previously remained legal are increasingly rejected by $A_{route}$,
- **residue incoherence:** residue signatures lose continuity across $\prec$-ordered cycles,
- **corridor misalignment:** reinforced routes cease to support stable re-entry into the prior basin.

Within this framework, fragmentation denotes loss of residue-coherent routing continuity across admissibility scope organization.

### 13.3 Scope Destabilization

Chapter 12 introduced scope persistence ($R_t \rightarrow Persistence_{scope}$) as the persistence of a routing arbitration boundary (admissibility scope), not a spatial container. **Scope destabilization** occurs when that boundary fails to remain stable under residue and reachability constraints:
- the coupled set of candidate signatures becomes inconsistent across cycles,
- reachability shifts so that formerly reachable candidates become inaccessible (or vice versa),
- admissibility gating becomes unstable, producing oscillatory or fragmentary routing.

Scope destabilization is therefore an admissibility-and-residue problem: the routing scope boundary no longer remains stable as a coherent organizational constraint.

### 13.4 Residue Fracture and Corridor Destabilization

Stable identity corridors require residue-coherent reinforcement: repeated ordered re-entry writes residue that stabilizes a route family. **Residue fracture** occurs when this continuity breaks. The routing organization no longer shares a coherent residue signature across cycles; residue persistence becomes locally inconsistent, and the prior recurrence basin loses its re-entry stability.

This coherence failure expresses as **corridor destabilization**: reinforced corridors stop functioning as stable, re-enterable route families. Corridor destabilization is not metaphysical annihilation; it is the procedural breakdown of reinforced accessibility structure. Practically, it appears as:
- corridor competition becomes unstable and non-reinforcing,
- re-entry pathways dissolve into diffuse or switching routing,
- arbitration cycles no longer preserve residue-coherent re-entry.

Preferred narrative compression (this chapter): **procedural breakdown of reinforced accessibility structure**.

We express the fracture relation as:
$$R_t^{-} \rightarrow Fragmentation_{scope}$$
Here $R_t^{-}$ denotes degraded residue persistence (fragmented or incoherent constraint persistence), and $Fragmentation_{scope}$ denotes scope fragmentation: the routing boundary splits into non-coherent sub-scopes that do not preserve a single stabilized recurrence basin.

### 13.5 Procedural Collapse

**Procedural collapse** is defined as failed continuation stabilization under admissibility constraints. It is not apocalyptic language; it is a formal statement: the configuration does not remain admissible as a stabilized recurrence basin.

Procedural collapse denotes loss of stabilized organizational persistence, not absolute procedural finality.

We represent collapse at the recursive identity stabilization condition as:
$$\Delta_R < \Theta_D \rightarrow Collapse_K$$
and equivalently as the failure of the recursive knot inequality:
$$\oint_{recursive} \Delta_R \not> \Theta_D$$

Collapse can occur through multiple admissibility-based routes consistent with the MPF-CORE collapse-mode boundaries (e.g., admissibility destabilization, symmetry fracture, or residue/constraint fragmentation). In each case, collapse is a failure of the conditions required for stabilized re-entry, not a claim about absolute termination of all continuation.

### 13.6 Recovery, Reformation, and Partial Re-Stabilization

Collapse is not required to be global or irreversible. Because continuation is distributed, failure can be local:
- a subset of corridors destabilizes while other corridors remain stable,
- a scope boundary fragments while some sub-scope remains re-enterable,
- a prior basin dissolves while a nearby basin becomes admissible and stabilizes.

We use **reformation basin** to denote a newly stabilized recurrence basin formed after drift or collapse events. Reformation is an admissibility-based process: routing arbitration re-stabilizes when residue coherence and admissible re-entry are re-established within a new (or repaired) scope.

Within this framework, a reformation basin denotes a newly stabilized admissibility region formed after collapse, drift, or residue-fracture events.

Because continuation organization remains distributed, partial stabilization may persist even when local routing structures destabilize.

Recovery therefore remains procedural: partial corridor recovery can occur when residue persistence resumes and admissibility gating re-opens consistent re-entry routes.

### 13.8 Transition to Procedural Ethics and Organizational Constraint

If persistence depends on admissibility, then interaction structure matters: some interaction patterns preserve residue-coherent re-entry, while others amplify fragmentation and collapse.
