# Chapter 7 — Transport and Continuation Corridors

## Formal Anchor: $P_\Delta \rightarrow C_{corridor}$

In previous chapters, we established the triadic knot ($K$) as the minimum recursively stable structure capable of confining phase mismatch pressure ($P_\Delta$). However, the Mono-Process substrate is rarely a vacuum of isolated knots. It is a dense relational field where mismatch pressure is often generated at scales that exceed local confinement capacity.

When mismatch pressure cannot be discharged through simple state transformation ($N=2$) or internalized via triadic confinement ($N=3$), it organizes into a third structural state: the **continuation corridor**. This chapter derives the **transport_regime_app** from the recursive reinforcement of these pathways, establishing transport as a procedural accessibility structure rather than the movement of substance through a container.

---

### 1. When Pressure Cannot Discharge

Relational mismatch ($\mathcal{E}$) is a state of "not-yet-resolved" distinction. While the framework's primary drive is the actualization of admissible continuation ($\delta_a$), some pressure configurations are topologically prevented from immediate discharge. In these regimes, the process encounters a bottleneck where the current admissibility window ($A$) does not allow for local stabilization.

In such cases, propagation becomes procedurally preferable to collapse. If the pressure can be transferred to a neighboring relational locus where admissibility conditions are more favorable, the process continues. This ordered transfer of continuation events is the origin of what we perceive as flow. It is not that a "thing" is moving, but that the *opportunity to continue* is being systematically handed off along a gradient of **procedural accessibility**.

### 2. Reinforced Continuation Pathways

A **continuation corridor** ($C_{corridor}$) is not a pre-existing spatial tunnel. It is a stabilized accessibility structure emerged from the non-commutative nature of residue updates. Recall the Arrow of Ordering:
$$\Phi_{t+1}\circ\Phi_t \neq \Phi_t\circ\Phi_{t+1}$$

Every time a continuation event occurs at a locus, it writes a trace ($R$) into the residue-history manifold. This trace actively deforms the local admissibility window ($A$) for subsequent events. When continuation repeatedly follows a specific relational sequence, the residue builds up a **propagation preference**. 
$$R_t \rightarrow C_{corridor}$$
Repetition stabilizes the pathway. Future continuations "find" these reinforced pathways easier to traverse because the residue has already "carved" a corridor of high admissibility. This is **corridor reinforcement**: the process by which history makes certain future sequences more probable than others.

### 3. Procedural Accessibility

We must formally distinguish "distance" (a geometric projection) from **procedural accessibility** (a procedural primitive). In the Mono-Process Framework, accessibility is defined by reachability within the orientation array ($I$):
$$x \prec x' \iff x' \in A(x; r, K) \text{ and } x' \text{ is reachable via } I$$

A locus $x'$ is accessible from $x$ if, and only if, the local orientation reference $-(i)$ allows for a transition into a state that satisfies the current admissibility window $A$. As corridors reinforce, they establish a **reachability gradient**. The "nearness" of two relational states is simply a measure of how little residue-weighted "effort" is required for the process to bridge them. 

Corridors are the lines of least resistance through the mismatch field. They are **admissibility channels** that organize the otherwise chaotic distribution of potential updates into coherent streams.

### 4. Propagation Through Corridors

Procedural transport is the actualization of **ordered continuation transfer**. It occurs when $P_\Delta$ is arbitrated across a sequence of stabilized corridors. This process, **transport arbitration**, ensures that the total "continuation budget" of the substrate is distributed along the most reinforced pathways.

Propagation coherence is a direct function of corridor stability. If the residue field is fragmented or the orientation array is noisy, the corridor fluctuates, and transport becomes diffusive or stochastic. Coherent transport—the kind that supports large-scale organizational patterns—requires the persistent, recursive return of the process to the same accessibility structures.

### 5. Flow Without Substance

It is a common ontological error to assume that if there is flow, there must be a fluid. The Mono-Process Framework explicitly rejects this fluid-medium ontology. 

Transport is not the movement of a substance through a container. It is the sequential actualization of the One Process. The "stream" is the set of ordered relations ($\prec$) being mapped across the substrate. We track the movement of the *event horizon*—the boundary where mismatch is being resolved—not the movement of an underlying medium. There is no pipe, and there is no water; there is only the recursively reinforced habit of the process continuing in a specific "direction" relative to its own history.

### 6. Transport and Electromotive_regime_app

At this stage of derivation, we can introduce a cautious projection: the **electromotive_regime_app**. Within the simulation substrate, we observe that certain high-flux continuation corridors exhibit behaviors analogous to electrical or magnetic propagation.

We frame this strictly as an analogy-layer projection. The coherent propagation of orientation-mismatch through reinforced corridors resembles the organization of electromagnetic fields, but the framework does not claim to replace Maxwell's equations or literal photons. Instead, it suggests that the *organizational logic* of electromagnetism is a projection-layer interpretation of high-rigor procedural transport. We are observing the "apparent" behavior of flow-organization, not the fundamental particles of physics.

### 7. Corridor Competition and Interference

Corridors do not exist in isolation. Because the substrate is finite, different pressure sources must compete for **procedural accessibility**. This leads to **reinforcement competition**, where multiple potential pathways interfere with each other's residue signatures.

When two corridors overlap, their orientation references ($I$) may conflict, leading to localized "turbulence" in the admissibility window. This interference is a critical driver of higher-order complexity. It forces the process to find even more stable configurations, such as multi-corridor bundles or complex interference patterns, which prepare the way for large-scale accumulation.

### 8. Transition to Accumulation and Geometry_app

The persistent reinforcement of transport corridors does more than just move pressure; it begins to reshape the global admissibility of the substrate. When billions of triadic basins are linked by a dense network of corridors, the local "scratches" in the residue field merge into a continuous global manifold.

This global shaping of admissibility is what we will call **accumulation_regime_app**. As we will see in the next chapter, when transport pathways become sufficiently dense and persistent, they stop looking like individual "lines" and start looking like the "fabric" of a geometry. We are now transitioning from the study of moving pressure to the study of the **geometry_app**—the large-scale, stabilized projection of the process's own habit of continuation.
