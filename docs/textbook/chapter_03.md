## 3.2 Non-Symmetry and Asymmetry

The most critical property of the distinction relation is its non-symmetry. The mismatch experienced from $S_1$ to $S_2$ is not necessarily equal to the mismatch from $S_2$ to $S_1$ [Source: TECH-NOTE-ASYM Sec 1].

**Formal Block 3.2.1: Non-Symmetry Condition**
$$ D(S_1|S_2) \neq D(S_2|S_1) $$

**Commentary:**
This non-symmetry is the generator of **orientation**. If all relations were symmetric, the process would have no "preferred direction" for continuation. The difference $\Delta D = |D(S_1|S_2) - D(S_2|S_1)|$ establishes a relational gradient that biases the admissibility of next steps.

## 3.4 The Floor Condition and Zero Distinction

As discussed in Chapter 1, $\mathcal{E} \neq 0$ is the root condition for the process. This implies a constraint on the distinction relation.

**Formal Block 3.4.1: The Floor Constraint**
$$ D(S_1|S_2) \geq \epsilon $$
$$ \epsilon > 0 $$

**Commentary:**
Within the framework, absolute zero distinction ($D = 0$) is an excluded state. The **floor $\epsilon$** (epsilon) represents the minimum possible distinguishability. This floor prevents the singular ratios that would occur if the process were to collapse into a state of total identity. If $D$ were to reach zero, orientation would become degenerate, and the recursive chain would terminate.
